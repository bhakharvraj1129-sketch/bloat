 Part I: The plan, explained simply

 1. What Bloat is
 Bloat is a mobile website (it can sit on the phone's home screen like an app) where adults
 record their bloating, meals and daily habits, see patterns in their own records, try simple
 experiments, and take a clear report to their doctor.

 The problem it solves. Most people with regular bloating have a guess about the cause ("it's
 dairy", "it's stress") but have never checked it. Doctors usually get vague memories instead of real
 records. Bloat replaces guessing with a simple, honest diary, and then helps people test their
 guesses safely.

 The promise: Track your bloating. Spot your possible triggers. Try simple experiments. Bring real
 data to your doctor.

 Why it's built this way. Nobody outside checks Bloat: no doctor, dietitian or lawyer. So safety is
 built into the design: the most cautious behaviour wherever a review would normally be used, NHS
 wording quoted word for word, and food swaps instead of cutting foods out. Bloat also stays a
 "general wellness" product: it shows information and patterns, but never makes medical decisions.
 Apps that make medical decisions (like Ada, which sorts symptoms by urgency) or claim to treat a
 condition (like Parallel or Cara Care for IBS) are regulated medical devices.

 6. Sign-up and setup
 Sign-up takes 4–5 minutes. Only age and location are required; anything skipped is saved as
 "unknown".

  1. Where do you live? USA, Canada, India or Other (Other goes to the waitlist).
  2. When to get medical help. Everyone reads this page and taps "I've read this".
  3. Sign up with an email code or Google, and confirm you're 18+.
  4. Three agreements: terms and privacy; storing health information; "Bloat is a wellness tracker,
    not medical advice, and not for emergencies".
  5. Location, time zone and units.
  6. About your bloating: what it feels like, how often, how long, when it's worst, other symptoms
    you often get.
  7. Your food: cuisines and preferences (vegetarian, vegan and so on).
  8. Health context: medicines and supplements with their type; pregnancy, breastfeeding,
    periods, or perimenopause and menopause (optional); age range and whether you have ovaries
    (optional, used only for the NHS doctor advice).
  9. Pick up to 3 habits to be asked about each morning.
  10. How Bloat works: 4 short cards, including "Bloat doesn't diagnose or treat anything".
 Optional profile cards appear in the first week (sleep, activity, usual meal times, routine, impact). Pro users add
 their health background later in Me.

 Rules that keep the numbers honest
  • A review is "confirmed" if done by the end of the next day; later ones are kept but marked "late"
    and left out of comparisons.
  • If the review says 2 but a 6 was logged, the app asks which is right.
  • The 7-day average needs at least 4 confirmed days and says how many it used.
  • How long bloating lasted is shown separately and never guessed.

 9. The meal AI (Pro)
 The AI does one job: it looks at a dish name or photo and lists only the ingredients that commonly
 cause gas or bloating. The user marks each one Had, Didn't have or Not sure. Anything left
 untouched counts as Not sure, never as "didn't have".

 It looks for 12 food groups, each backed by a public source: dairy with lactose; wheat and whole
 grains; onion and garlic; beans and lentils; fruit sugars; "-ol" sweeteners; cabbage-family
 vegetables; fried or fatty food; spicy food; fizzy drinks; caffeine; alcohol.

  • Every list says "AI suggestion, may be wrong".
  • If the user doesn't know, they tap "I don't know what was in it" and nothing is guessed. A single
    Not sure item (say garlic in a restaurant sauce) only affects garlic; the day still counts for
    everything else. For meals not made at home, anything the AI didn't list is treated as unknown,
    because restaurant dishes hide ingredients.
  • "Assume the usual recipe" saves a guess that's shown in history but not used in comparisons.
  • Saved dishes make repeat meals one tap, and can include the restaurant name.
  • Photos have location data removed and are deleted after the check unless the user chooses to
    keep them.
  • No calories, ever.
  • The AI never sees symptoms, health information or who the user is.
  • Before launch, it must pass a test on 300 dishes (US, Indian, Canadian and restaurant) and 100
    photos, counting missed ingredients, wrong ones and correct "can't tell" answers separately.
  • Cost: about 4–5 US cents per Pro user a month.

 After about two months most people know their patterns, and logging every meal starts to feel like a

 11. Experiments (Pro)
 Experiments turn a guess into a personal test. Habit experiments are open to every Pro user; food
 swaps have extra safety steps.

 Habit experiments (7 days)
 Eat more slowly · No gum or hard candy · No straws · Fewer fizzy drinks · Walk 10–15 minutes after
 meals · Slow breathing 3 minutes, 3 times a day · Two kiwifruit a day (for people logging
 constipation who have no food allergy).

 Food swaps (2 weeks + 1 week adding back)
 Instead of cutting a food out, the user swaps it for a named equivalent, so nothing is missing from
 their diet: lactose-free milk and yogurt for regular dairy; garlic-infused oil and chives for onion and
 garlic; smaller or rinsed portions of beans and lentils; regular sweets instead of sugar-free ones;
 orange, grapes or kiwi instead of apples, pears and honey. The swap stops automatically at 2 weeks,
 adding the food back for 1 week is required, and there are at most 2 swaps in 6 months. Wheat and
 multi-food (FODMAP) programmes are never offered, because they normally need professional
 supervision.

 Safety steps for food swaps
  • Before starting, four questions: pregnant or breastfeeding? eating disorder now or before, or
    worried about food? coeliac disease, food allergy, Crohn's or colitis? losing weight without trying?
    Any yes means food swaps aren't offered, with a kind explanation.
  • Every week, two questions: worrying about food more? skipping meals or eating with others
    because of food? A yes pauses the experiment and shows a helpline (USA: National Alliance for
    Eating Disorders 1-866-662-1235; Canada: NEDIC 1-866-633-4220; India: Tele-MANAS 14416).
  • Every screen says "Stop if you feel worse" and links to When to get medical help.

 Results
    "During the experiment your average Bloat Score was 3.9 (6 days). In the 7 days before it was 5.2 (7
    days). This is a pattern in your own records, not proof that the change caused it."

 With too few days, the app says so instead of showing numbers.

 12. Daily cards and Learn library (Pro)
 Each day a Pro user gets one card, alternating between a tip (for example "eat more slowly", "check
 sugar-free labels", "no big meal right before bed") and a mind–body activity (walk after a meal,
 slow breathing, tummy massage from right to left, gentle stretch, wind-down for sleep).

  • Cards are picked from what the user logged, and say why they appeared.
  • Each card restates one sentence from a public source and links to it; no card adds advice of its
    own.
  • If every suitable card has been shown in the last 2 weeks, the one shown longest ago comes
    back. 20 cards are enough for launch; 40 is the goal.
  • Cards that don't suit someone are hidden based on published guidance (for example, no tummy
    massage after recent surgery).
  • Users can mark a card helpful, no difference or worse. "Worse" hides it and is reviewed by you.

  • The Learn library explains why bloating happens, the gut–brain link, what FODMAPs are, what the
    evidence says about probiotics (experts don't recommend them for bloating), and how to prepare
    for a doctor visit.

 or collecting genetic data or ethnicity.

 14. Privacy
  • Each user's records are locked to them by the database itself.
  • No name, phone or photo required (an email still identifies the account, so it's never called
    anonymous).
  • Separate agreements for each use; any can be withdrawn.
  • Download everything any time, free. Delete the account and everything goes, including from
    backups.
  • Doctor sharing needs a preview, consent and an expiry date.
  • Each country's privacy law is covered: US federal and state health-data rules, Canada's PIPEDA
    and Quebec's Law 25, India's DPDP Act.
